Cold Agglutinin Disease

Workup, treatment and transfusion protocols at the bedside. Works offline.

Critical · Read first

Keep it warm

  • Specimen handling keep blood samples at 37–40 °C from the moment of draw until serum/plasma is separated. Pre-warm tubes, transport in a 37 °C water bath / heat block or warmed container, and tell the lab it is a cold agglutinin sample. Cooling causes in-vitro agglutination and adsorption of the antibody onto RBCs, giving a falsely low titer and spurious CBC results.
  • No cold IV fluids or blood warm ALL infusions and blood products with an approved in-line blood/fluid warmer. Never use improvised methods (microwave, hot water, radiators).
  • Keep the patient warm warm room, blankets, cover extremities/head; avoid cold exposure, ice packs and cold drinks. Avoid perioperative hypothermia.
  • Do not withhold urgently needed transfusion because of crossmatch difficulty; discuss with the transfusion service and give warmed blood.

Diagnostic criteria (consensus, e.g. First International Consensus Meeting / Berentsen et al.): chronic hemolysis + polyspecific DAT positive + monospecific DAT strongly positive for C3d (IgG negative or weakly positive) + cold agglutinin titer ≥64 at 4 °C, with no overt malignancy or infection. Etiology

Primary CAD vs. Secondary CAS

A clonal B-cell disorder, or a syndrome secondary to infection or malignancy.

Cold agglutinin disease (CAD) is a clonal B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder (LPD). Cold agglutinin syndrome (CAS) is the same phenotype secondary to another condition. Both are complement-mediated (classical pathway) hemolytic anemias.

FeaturePrimary CADSecondary CAS
Underlying causeClonal B-cell LPD of the bone marrow ("CAD-associated LPD"); usually low-grade, distinct from LPL (typically MYD88 L265P negative)Infection (Mycoplasma pneumoniae, EBV, CMV, SARS-CoV-2), aggressive lymphoma (e.g. DLBCL), other malignancy, rarely autoimmune disease
AntibodyMonoclonal IgM-κ (>90%), usually anti-I; heavy chain often encoded by IGHV4-34Polyclonal IgM after infection (Mycoplasma → anti-I; EBV → anti-i); monoclonal if lymphoma-driven
Onset & courseOlder adults (median ~65–70 y); chronic, relapsing; worse in winter and with febrile illness / surgeryAcute, often 1–3 weeks into infection; usually self-limited over weeks once the trigger resolves
Treatment focusThermal protection; complement inhibition (sutimlimab) or B-cell-directed therapy if symptomaticTreat the trigger (antimicrobial, lymphoma therapy); supportive care, warm transfusion if needed

Mycoplasma pneumoniae

Most classic infectious cause. Anti-I cold agglutinins are common in Mycoplasma pneumonia but clinically significant hemolysis is uncommon. Hemolysis typically appears in the 2nd–3rd week and resolves within weeks.

EBV / infectious mononucleosis

Anti-i specificity. Usually transient. Also consider CMV, SARS-CoV-2 and other viral triggers.

Aggressive lymphoma / other malignancy

Suspect with B symptoms, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, rapidly rising LDH out of proportion to hemolysis, or atypical presentation. Image (CT/PET) and biopsy as indicated; therapy is directed at the lymphoma.

Clinical clues to CAD: acrocyanosis and Raynaud-like symptoms in cold, hemoglobinuria after cold exposure, fatigue; anemia ranges from mild to transfusion-dependent. Circulatory symptoms are caused by RBC agglutination (not complement) and may persist despite complement blockade.
Treatment

Acute & Chronic Management

From thermal protection to complement inhibition and B-cell-directed therapy.

1Thermal protection (all patients)

  • Avoid cold exposure: warm clothing, gloves, hats, scarves; warm indoor environment; avoid cold food/drinks.
  • Warm IV fluids and blood products with an in-line warmer during any admission or procedure.
  • Folic acid supplementation for chronic hemolysis is common practice.
  • Treat intercurrent infections promptly (febrile illness can trigger hemolytic crises via acute-phase complement increase).
  • Mild, stable anemia without symptoms may need no drug therapy.

2Acute severe hemolysis

  • Warmed RBC transfusion as needed; keep patient warm; treat triggers.
  • Consider rapid-acting complement inhibition (sutimlimab; eculizumab has been used off-label).
  • Therapeutic plasma exchange removes IgM rapidly but transiently. Use as a bridge (e.g. before urgent surgery); warm the circuit and replacement fluid. Albumin is the usual replacement fluid (plasma supplies complement).

3Sutimlimab (anti-C1s)

Humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody against C1s that blocks the classical complement pathway upstream of C3 and C4. Approved (FDA 2022, EMA/others thereafter) to treat hemolysis in adults with CAD. Rapid effect: hemoglobin rises within 1–2 weeks and transfusion needs fall.

ItemDetails
Dosing (label)IV, weight-based: 39 to <75 kg: 6,500 mg; ≥75 kg: 7,500 mg. Weekly for 2 doses, then every 2 weeks. Verify against current local label.
ResponseImproves Hb, bilirubin, fatigue (CARDINAL, CADENZA trials). Does not remove the clone or the cold agglutinin; does not reliably improve agglutination-driven circulatory symptoms (acrocyanosis).
DurationContinuous therapy; hemolysis recurs within weeks after stopping. Monitor for recurrence after discontinuation.
RisksSerious infection with encapsulated bacteria; infusion/hypersensitivity reactions; possible development of autoimmune disease (e.g. SLE) noted in label. Monitor for infection.

Choice: complement inhibition gives rapid hemolysis control (bridge or long-term), while B-cell therapy aims at durable remission without continuous dosing. They can be sequenced or combined according to severity and patient fitness.

5Why steroids & splenectomy generally fail

  • Corticosteroids: low response rates; responses typically need unacceptably high doses, and steroid exposure adds infection, bone and metabolic toxicity. Not recommended for maintenance. (Unlike warm AIHA.)
  • Splenectomy: C3b-opsonized RBCs are cleared mainly by Kupffer cells in the liver (extravascular), plus some intravascular lysis via the membrane attack complex. The spleen is not the main site of destruction, so splenectomy is ineffective and adds infection risk.
  • Other immunosuppressants (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide alone, IVIG) have little supporting evidence.

6Other considerations

  • CAD carries an increased risk of venous and arterial thrombosis; stay alert and use thromboprophylaxis when admitted or post-op per standard risk assessment.
  • Monitor Hb, LDH, bilirubin, reticulocytes; repeat clonal assessment if the course changes.
  • Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents may help selected patients with inadequate reticulocytosis.
Protocols

Transfusion & Surgery Protocols

Warm everything: blood, fluids, the patient and the bypass circuit.

Transfusion

  • Blood warmer: use an approved in-line blood warmer for all RBC units and IV fluids. Infuse slowly while monitoring for hemolysis.
  • Warm the patient (room, blankets, especially extremities) during and after transfusion.
  • Compatibility testing: cold autoantibody can interfere with ABO typing and crossmatch. The lab may use pre-warmed (37 °C) technique, washing with warm saline, or autoadsorption to exclude underlying alloantibodies. Inform the lab of the diagnosis.
  • Washed RBCs: optional and rarely needed. The historic rationale (removing donor complement) is unproven; warmed standard units are generally sufficient.
  • Do not delay life-saving transfusion for full serologic workup; give warmed, least-incompatible units in consultation with the transfusion service.
  • Expect a shorter survival of transfused RBCs during active hemolysis; set realistic Hb targets.

Surgery & anesthesia

  • Notify anesthesia, surgery, perfusion and transfusion service pre-op; check titer and thermal amplitude.
  • Maintain strict normothermia: warmed OR, forced-air warming, warmed IV fluids, blood, irrigation and inspired gases.
  • Avoid induced hypothermia where possible.
  • For high-titer / high-thermal-amplitude antibodies before major elective surgery, consider pre-op plasma exchange or complement inhibition (sutimlimab) to reduce hemolysis risk.

Cardiac surgery / cardiopulmonary bypass

  • Avoid cold cardioplegia and systemic hypothermia. Use normothermic (warm) bypass and warm-blood cardioplegia when feasible.
  • If hypothermia is unavoidable, perfusion may screen the circuit/cardioplegia for agglutination and plan plasma exchange pre-op.
  • Watch for agglutination in cardioplegia lines, coronary microcirculation, and post-op hemolysis.
Laboratory

Lab Interpretation & Pitfalls

How cold agglutinins distort routine results, and what to expect.

TestTypical finding / pitfall
Automated CBCAgglutinates counted as single large cells: falsely low RBC count and hematocrit, falsely high MCV and very high MCHC (often >36 g/dL). Re-run after warming the sample to 37 °C.
Direct antiglobulin testStrongly positive for C3d; IgG negative (or weakly positive in a minority).
Cold agglutinin titer≥64 at 4 °C supports CAD; titers in CAD are often ≥512. Low titers can be seen in healthy people. False low if sample cooled before separation.
Thermal amplitudeHighest temperature at which the antibody agglutinates RBCs. Antibodies reacting at ≥28–30 °C are clinically significant; thermal amplitude correlates with severity better than titer.
Peripheral smearRBC agglutination (clumps) on a room-temperature smear, which disperses when the slide/sample is warmed. Spherocytes are less prominent than in warm AIHA.
SPEP / IFEMonoclonal IgM-κ in most primary CAD; serum must be separated at 37 °C or the M-protein may be underestimated.
Complement (C3, C4)C4 often low (consumption); C3 may be low-normal. Acute-phase rises in C3 during febrile illness can worsen hemolysis.
ChemistryVisible agglutination can interfere with some analyzers; serum may show hemolysis if drawn/handled cold.
